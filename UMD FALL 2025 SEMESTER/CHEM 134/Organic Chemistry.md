### Organic Chemistry

**Organic chemistry** is a branch of chemistry that studies compounds that contain carbon, in combination with hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, sulfur, silicon, and halides. Oxides and carbonates are considered inorganic, so $CO_2$, for example, is considered inorganic.

### Writing chemical formulas

The **molecular formula** shows the kinds of atoms in the molecule, but it does not show how the atoms are attached. For example, $C_4 H_{10}$ can have more than one structure.

**Structural formulas** show how the atoms are attached. For example, butane ($C_4 H_{10}$) can have an n-butane or an isobutane structure.

**Condensed structural formulas** list atoms in order of how they are attached. Parentheses are used to indicate more than one identical group attached to the same previous central atom. For example, for n-butane, the structural formula would be "$CH_{3} (CH_{2})_2 CH_{3}$," while for isobutane, it would be "$CH (CH_3)_3$."

The **carbon skeleton formula** is condensed and convenient to write. We know how many hydrogens there are without drawing them because  each carbon forms four single bonds. Each vertex represents a carbon atom, and each edge represents a bond between carbon atoms.

### Isomerism

**Isomers** are different molecules with the same molecular formula. There are two main types:
1. **Structural isomers** (or constitutional isomers) are isomers that have a different pattern of atom attachment. N-Butane and isobutane are examples of structural isomers.
2. **Stereoisomers** are isomers with the same pattern of atom attachments, but the atoms have a different spatial orientation.

Optical isomers are nonsuperimposable mirror images of each other. We describe these molecules as *chiral*, meaning it has a *chiral center*, which is an atom (usually carbon) that has four different substituents. A chiral molecule will have at least two enantiomers (but can have more if it has more than one chiral center).

Enantiomers have the same chemical reactivity in a nonchiral environment, in which everything is randomly oriented. However, in a chiral environment they may exhibit different behaviors. This is important in biological systems that contain enzymes, which have very complex structures. Enzymes interact with very specific molecules and at specific orientations. Thus, they often only operate on specific enantiomers.

Enantiomers rotate plane-polarized light in different directions, so we can indicate the enantiomer with the direction that it rotates light.

D- Dextrorotatory = rotates the plane to the right
L - Levorotatory = rotates the plane to the left

### Hydrocarbons

The simplest type of organic compound is a **hydrocarbon**, a compound that contains only $C$ and $H$ atoms in their structure. Carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativity, so they are nonpolar and therefore insoluble in water.

Hydrocarbons are very diverse and can form straight-chain, branch, or ring (cyclic) structures.

- **Aliphatic** hydrocarbons are based on chains of carbons, and there are three types:
	- **Alkanes**: Only single-bonded carbon atoms; saturated
	- **Alkenes**: Contain at least one double bond; unsaturated
	- **Alkynes**: Contain at least one triple bond; unsaturated
- **Aromatic** hydrocarbons (benzene ring)

### Alkanes

Alkanes are also known as paraffins. Their structure is saturated, which means that carbon only forms single bonds (i.e., it cannot take any more bonds). They are nonpolar, and thus only form dispersion forces.

### Alkenes and Alkynes

Alkenes and alkynes are known as unsaturated hydrocarbons. **Alkenes** contain $C=C$ double bonds and form a trigonal planar structure, and are also know as olefins. **Alkynes** contain $C \equiv C$ triple bonds and are also known as acetylenes, and they have a linear structure.

##### Naming alkenes and alkynes

1. Find the longest continuous carbon chain with the double or triple bond. Combine parent prefix with the suffix "-ene" for an alkene and "-yne" for an alkyne
2. Number the chain from end closest to a multiple bond. Number them such that the first carbon of the multiple bond has a lower number.
3. Number in front of main name indicates first carbon of multiple bond.
4. Name branches as alkyl groups. Locate each branch by preceding its name with the carbon number on the chain.

### Cis-trans isomerism

**Geometric isomers** are a type of stereoisomer that applies to molecules with double bonds. The rotation around a double bond is highly restricted, so if groups have different spatial orientation about the double bond they cannot easily convert into the other form.

A molecule has geometric isomers if it has a rigid structure (such as a carbon-carbon double bond or a ring), and each of the two carbons has two different groups attached to it.

When like groups on the doubly bonded carbons are on the same side of the double bond, we call this a *cis* configuration. When like groups on the doubly bonded carbons on opposite sides, we call this a *trans* configuration.

### Function Groups

A **functional group** is a group of atoms that shows a characteristic influence on the physical and chemical properties of the molecule. Generally, the reactions that a compound will perform are determined by what functional groups it has. Some major types include alcohols, ketones, aldehydes, carboxylic acids, amines, esters, and ethers.

**Alcohols** are compounds with at least one -$OH$ group (hydroxyl group). Alcohols can form hydrogen bonds because of the -$OH$ group, and they are generally polar and water-soluble. As the hydrocarbon part of the molecule gets longer or larger, the alcohol will become less water-soluble.

**Carboxylic acids** are weak organic acids with all the characteristics of a weak acid, such as having a sour taste. They contain both carbonyl ($C = O$) and a hydroxyl (-$OH$) group. Like all weak acids, carboxylic acids become partially deprotonated, giving up a proton and becoming negatively charged. We call the deprotonated group carboxylate.

**Amines** behave as weak bases. All amines contain a nitrogen atom with a lone pair and are bonded to three substituent groups. The nitrogen lone pair is where the amine can accept a proton.
